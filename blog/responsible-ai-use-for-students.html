Using AI writing tools responsibly: a guide for students

How students can use AI writing tools within academic integrity rules: finding your policy, lower-risk uses, disclosure, citing AI and keeping records.

AI writing tools can help you learn: explaining a tricky concept, suggesting counterarguments or pointing out where a paragraph is unclear. They can also get you into serious trouble if you use them in ways your course doesn’t allow. The difference usually isn’t the tool. It’s how and where you use it.

This guide explains how to find out what’s permitted, which uses are generally lower-risk, how to disclose and cite AI assistance, and what to do about AI detectors.

Why the rules are so different everywhere

There’s no single rule for AI in education. Policies can differ between countries, institutions, departments, individual courses and even assignments in the same course. One instructor may encourage AI brainstorming; another may ban AI entirely for a take-home essay because the assignment is designed to assess your own writing.

So the most important question isn’t “Is AI allowed?” but “Is this particular use allowed for this particular assignment?”

How to find your policy

  1. Check the assignment brief and syllabus. Many courses now include a specific statement on generative AI.
  2. Read your institution’s academic integrity policy. Look for sections on “unauthorised assistance”, “contract cheating” or “generative AI”.
  3. Ask your instructor if anything is unclear. Asking in writing, for example by email, gives you a record of the answer.
  4. Don’t assume one course’s rules apply to another.

Common uses, from lower to higher risk

This is a general guide only. Your course policy always takes priority.

UseTypical statusWhy
Explaining a concept you’re studyingOften allowedSimilar to using a textbook or tutor. Verify the explanation against course materials.
Brainstorming topics or counterargumentsOften allowed, may need disclosureThe ideas still need your research and judgement.
Feedback on clarity or grammar in your own draftDepends on the courseSome courses assess language skills directly.
Rewriting or paraphrasing your paragraphsDepends; often restrictedThe wording is no longer entirely yours.
Generating an outline you follow closelyOften restrictedThe structure of the argument is part of what’s assessed.
Generating full paragraphs or essays you submitUsually not allowedTypically treated as unauthorised assistance or misconduct.

Editing tools, including the Text Humanize humanizer, fall into the “rewriting” category. Only use them on coursework where your instructor permits AI editing, and disclose that use if asked.

How to disclose AI use

When disclosure is required, or when you’re unsure, a short, specific statement is best. Say which tool you used, what for, and what you did with the output. For example:

I used ChatGPT to suggest possible counterarguments to my thesis. I researched and evaluated the two I included myself. I also used an AI editing tool to suggest clearer wording for my introduction, and reviewed each change. All analysis and conclusions are my own.

Follow any format your instructor asks for, such as a note at the end of the essay or an appendix containing your prompts.

How to cite AI tools

Major style guides have published guidance on citing generative AI, including the APA Style blog and the MLA Style Center. Formats differ, so use the style your course requires, and check whether your instructor wants prompts included. Our step-by-step guide shows how to cite ChatGPT and other AI tools in APA, MLA and Chicago style.

Remember that AI isn’t a reliable source of facts. Cite the AI tool for content it generated, but find and cite real, verifiable sources for any facts, statistics or quotations you use. When you draw on other writers’ ideas, paraphrase and cite them properly.

Check everything AI tells you

AI tools can state wrong information confidently and produce references that look real but don’t exist. Before you rely on anything an AI tool gives you:

  • look up every source it mentions and confirm it says what’s claimed;
  • check facts against course materials or credible sources;
  • be especially careful with numbers, dates, quotations and legal or medical information.

Keep a record of your work

Good records protect you and make disclosure easy:

  • Write in a document with version history (Google Docs, Microsoft Word with autosave)
  • Save your notes, outlines and research sources
  • Keep copies of AI conversations you used, if AI was permitted
  • Save earlier drafts rather than overwriting them

Do learning platforms detect AI?

Students often ask whether Canvas, Blackboard or Moodle can “detect ChatGPT”. Learning management systems themselves are mainly places to submit and grade work. What matters is which tools your institution connects to them. Some institutions enable AI-writing indicators in services such as Turnitin; others have chosen to turn such features off. Blackboard’s SafeAssign is primarily a text-matching tool for identifying overlap with existing sources.

But focusing on detection misses the point. Detectors are unreliable in both directions, and misconduct cases can also rest on other evidence, such as a writing style that doesn’t match your previous work or an inability to discuss your essay. The only dependable approach is to use AI in ways that are allowed.

If you’re wrongly flagged

AI detectors do flag genuine student work, and some groups of writers, including non-native English speakers, are flagged more often. If it happens to you, stay calm, keep your drafts, and read our guide to responding to AI detector false positives.

Why doing the thinking yourself still matters

Assignments exist to build skills: researching, structuring an argument, and expressing ideas clearly. If an AI tool does that work, you get the grade without the skill, and the skill is what you’ll need in exams, interviews and your career. Used well, AI can speed up learning: it can explain, question and give feedback. Used as a replacement for your own thinking, it quietly takes away the thing you came to learn.
