How to use AI to write a cover letter that doesn’t sound generic

Use AI to draft a cover letter without sounding generic: what to give the tool, a prompt you can adapt, how to add real evidence and a final checklist.

AI tools can produce a tidy cover letter in seconds. That’s exactly the problem: hiring managers read a lot of tidy, interchangeable letters. A cover letter only works when it’s obviously about you and this job.

This guide shows how to use AI for the parts it’s good at (structure, a first draft, tightening wording) while you supply the part it can’t: real evidence of why you’re a good fit.

Why AI cover letters sound generic

An AI tool knows nothing about your experience or the company unless you tell it. With little to go on, it fills the gaps with the most common cover-letter language:

  • “I am writing to express my interest in the position of…”
  • “I am a highly motivated and passionate professional…”
  • “…with a proven track record of success”
  • “I am confident that my skills make me an ideal candidate”

None of these phrases is wrong, but none tells the reader anything. The fix isn’t a cleverer prompt. It’s better input.

Step 1: Gather your raw material

Before you open an AI tool, spend ten minutes collecting:

  • The job ad. Highlight the three or four requirements that matter most.
  • Three achievements from your work, studies or projects, with results you can back up.
  • Why this organisation. Something specific: a product you use, a project they published, how they work.
  • Practical details such as availability, location or a referral, if relevant.

Step 2: Match your evidence to the job

A simple table keeps the letter focused on what the employer asked for:

What the job asks forYour evidence
Manages social media channelsRan the student society’s Instagram for two years and grew event sign-ups
Writes clear reportsWrote the monthly volunteer update read by the whole team
Comfortable with dataBuilt a spreadsheet dashboard to track campaign results

Use your own examples, of course. If you can’t match a requirement, don’t invent evidence; focus on the ones you can support.

Step 3: Use a prompt that limits the AI to your facts

Paste your material into a prompt like this and adapt it:

I’m applying for [job title] at [organisation]. Here is the job description: [paste].

Here is my evidence, matched to the key requirements: [paste your table or notes]. Here is why I want to work there: [notes].

Write a cover letter of about 300 words in a warm, confident, professional tone. Open with why I’m interested in this specific role. Use only the facts I’ve provided. If something is missing, write [ADD DETAIL] instead of inventing it. Avoid these phrases: passionate, proven track record, highly motivated, dynamic, I am writing to express my interest.

The instruction to use only your facts matters. Without it, AI tools may add plausible-sounding achievements or skills you don’t have.

Step 4: Edit the draft for specifics

Treat the output as a first draft. Go through it paragraph by paragraph and replace anything general with something concrete.

Generic

I am a highly motivated marketing professional with a proven track record of driving engagement and a passion for creative campaigns.

Specific

For the last two years I’ve run the social channels for a local charity, planning a monthly campaign calendar and turning our event photos into posts that brought in new volunteers.

The specific version is longer, but every word gives the reader a reason to keep going. Also check that the opening sentence names the role and gives a real reason for applying, not a formula.

Step 5: Get the structure and length right

Most cover letters work well with a simple shape:

  1. Opening: the role, and a genuine reason this job appeals to you.
  2. One or two evidence paragraphs: your strongest examples, linked to what the employer needs.
  3. Close: a short summary of what you’d bring, and a polite line about next steps.

Hiring managers are busy, so keep it to a single page. Three or four short paragraphs is usually plenty. If the employer gives specific instructions, such as a word limit or questions to answer, follow those instead.

Step 6: Check facts, tone and fit

  • Every claim is true, and you could talk about it in an interview
  • The organisation’s name, the job title and any names are spelled correctly
  • No placeholder text such as [ADD DETAIL] is left behind
  • It sounds like you when you read it aloud
  • It’s tailored to this job, not a letter you could send anywhere

If a few sentences still sound stiff, you can paste them into the Text Humanize editor, choose Cover letter under “Writing for”, and compare the changes before keeping any.

Phrases worth cutting

Search your draft for these and replace them with evidence or delete them:

  • “I believe I would be a great fit” (show why instead)
  • “team player”, “hard-working”, “detail-oriented” (give an example that proves it)
  • “I look forward to hearing from you at your earliest convenience” (a simple “Thank you for considering my application” is enough)
  • “In today’s competitive job market…” (delete)

A note on honesty

Using AI to help draft and polish a cover letter is common and generally acceptable, unless an application tells you otherwise. What matters is that the content is accurate and yours. Interviews will test what you wrote, so never let a tool add qualifications, results or experience you don’t have.
