How to paraphrase properly without plagiarizing

How to paraphrase a source properly: understand it, rewrite it in your own words and structure, cite it, and avoid patchwriting, with worked examples.

Paraphrasing means expressing someone else’s idea in your own words. Done well, it shows you understand a source and lets you blend evidence smoothly into your writing. Done badly, it can count as plagiarism, even if you didn’t mean to copy.

This guide explains what a proper paraphrase looks like, a reliable method for writing one, and how paraphrasing tools and AI fit in.

Paraphrasing, quoting and summarizing

What it isWhen to use it
QuotingThe author’s exact words, in quotation marksWhen the precise wording matters, such as a definition or a memorable phrase
ParaphrasingA specific idea restated in your own words and structure, about the same lengthWhen you need the detail of an idea but not the author’s wording
SummarizingThe main points of a longer passage, much shorterWhen you need the big picture, not the detail

All three need a citation. A paraphrase is still someone else’s idea, even when every word is yours.

What goes wrong: patchwriting

The most common paraphrasing problem is keeping the original sentence structure and swapping in synonyms. Writing researchers call this patchwriting. It often happens when writers are still getting to grips with a difficult source, but many institutions treat it as plagiarism because the structure and much of the expression still belong to the original author.

Here is a source passage written for this guide:

Sleep plays an important role in memory. During deep sleep, the brain appears to replay and strengthen information learned during the day, which may explain why students who sleep well after studying often remember more than those who stay up late.

Patchwriting

Sleep has a significant part in memory. In deep sleep, the brain seems to replay and reinforce knowledge learned in the day, which might explain why learners who sleep well after studying frequently recall more than those who stay awake late.

Proper paraphrase

Getting a good night’s sleep after studying may improve recall, because the brain seems to consolidate the day’s learning during deep sleep (Author, Year).

The weak version follows the original sentence by sentence and changes individual words. The strong version restructures the idea, keeps the meaning, uses genuinely different wording and includes a citation.

A five-step method for paraphrasing

  1. Read until you understand. Read the passage more than once. If you can’t explain it to a friend, you’re not ready to paraphrase it.
  2. Note the key ideas. Jot down the main point and any essential details in a few words, not full sentences.
  3. Write from memory. Close or cover the source and write the idea as you would explain it, using your notes.
  4. Compare with the original. Check that the meaning is accurate and that you haven’t reused distinctive phrases or the same sentence structure. Put any unique phrase you do keep in quotation marks.
  5. Cite the source in the style your course or publication requires.
A quick test

Place your paraphrase next to the original. If you can match your sentences to theirs one by one, or several distinctive words appear in the same order, rewrite it again.

Keep the meaning accurate

A paraphrase must say what the author said, no more and no less. Watch out for:

  • Overstating certainty. If the source says a finding “may explain” something, don’t write that it “proves” it.
  • Dropping conditions. “In some cases”, “for younger participants” and similar limits often matter.
  • Adding your own opinion. Keep your interpretation separate, for example in the next sentence: “This suggests that…”.

When to quote instead

Some wording can’t be improved or shouldn’t be changed. Quote directly when you’re analysing the author’s exact language, citing a definition or a legal text, or using a phrase so distinctive that rewording would lose its force. Keep quotations short and explain them in your own words.

Paraphrasing tools, AI and plagiarism

Paraphrasing tools and AI rewriters change wording automatically. That can be useful when you are editing your own writing, but it creates risks with other people’s work:

  • Automated rewording often produces patchwriting: the same structure with swapped words
  • It can subtly change the meaning of the original
  • It doesn’t remove the need to cite the source
  • Many schools treat disguising a source with a rewording tool as academic misconduct

Paraphrasing checklist

  • I understand the passage well enough to explain it without looking
  • My version uses different structure and wording, not just synonyms
  • The meaning, including any limits or uncertainty, is accurate
  • Any distinctive phrase I kept is in quotation marks
  • I included a citation in the required style
