AI detector false positives: why they happen and what to do

Why AI detectors flag human writing, who is most at risk, how to protect yourself with good writing habits, and what to do if your work is wrongly flagged.

Being told your own writing “looks AI-generated” is stressful, especially when a grade, a job or a client relationship is on the line. False positives are a known limitation of AI detection, not a rare glitch. This guide explains why they happen, who is most affected, how to reduce the risk, and what to do if it happens to you.

What a false positive is

A false positive is when a detector labels human-written text as AI-generated. It happens because detectors don’t detect AI directly. They estimate probability from patterns in the writing, and those patterns also appear in plenty of human text. Our explainer on how AI detection works covers the mechanics in detail.

Why human writing gets flagged

Predictable, formal writing

Detectors often treat very predictable text as a sign of AI. But much good human writing is predictable by design: lab reports, legal summaries, instructions, and essays that follow a structure students were taught to use.

Limited vocabulary

Writers working in a second language often rely on common words and familiar sentence structures. That lowers the “surprise” detectors look for. A 2023 Stanford study found that several popular detectors misclassified more than half of a set of TOEFL essays written by non-native English speakers as AI-generated (Liang et al., 2023).

Common topics and phrasing

If an assignment asks hundreds of people to write about the same subject, many responses will use similar terms, examples and arguments. That makes each one look more “typical”.

Short passages

A few sentences don’t give a detector enough material to measure, so short texts produce less reliable results in both directions.

Heavy use of editing tools

Aggressive automated editing, such as accepting every grammar or style suggestion, can smooth out the natural irregularities in your writing and make it more uniform. That doesn’t mean you shouldn’t use a spell checker, but it’s one more reason a score isn’t evidence on its own.

Who is most at risk

  • Non-native English writers, for the vocabulary reasons above
  • Students in technical and scientific fields, where precise, conventional phrasing is expected
  • Writers who were taught formulaic structures, such as the five-paragraph essay
  • Anyone submitting short responses, such as discussion posts or brief answers

How to protect yourself

The best protection isn’t a trick for lowering a score. It’s a clear record of how you worked.

  • Write in a tool with version history, such as Google Docs or Microsoft Word with autosave, so the development of your work is recorded.
  • Keep your notes, outlines and sources. Research notes and early drafts are strong evidence of your process.
  • Know the rules. Read your syllabus or workplace policy on AI tools, and ask if it’s unclear.
  • Disclose any AI use that’s permitted, briefly describing what the tool did. See our student guide for examples.
  • Let your own voice show. Specific examples, your own reasoning and references to class discussions make work recognisably yours.
Don’t try to “fix” a score with a rewriting tool

If your original work is wrongly flagged, running it through a paraphraser or humanizer won’t prove you wrote it. It can make matters worse by creating a version that no longer matches your drafts. Your process evidence is what counts.

What to do if your work is flagged

1. Stay calm and don’t delete anything

A flag is the start of a conversation, not a verdict. Keep every draft, file and note, and don’t edit the original document’s history.

2. Ask what the concern is based on

Politely ask which tool was used, what it reported, and which passages were highlighted. Ask whether any other evidence is being considered and what the next steps are.

3. Gather your evidence

  • Version history showing the document developing over time
  • Outlines, notes, research sources and earlier drafts
  • Previous work that shows a similar writing style
  • Any permitted AI use and how you disclosed it

4. Explain your process

Be ready to talk through how you researched and wrote the piece: why you chose your sources, how your argument developed, and what you changed between drafts. Being able to discuss your work in detail is persuasive.

5. Refer to the tool’s own limitations

Detector makers acknowledge that their tools can be wrong. Turnitin, for example, advises that its AI writing indicator shouldn’t be used as the sole basis for action. OpenAI withdrew its own AI text classifier in 2023, citing low accuracy. It’s reasonable to point this out respectfully.

6. Use the formal process if needed

If the matter goes further, follow your institution’s academic integrity or appeals procedure. Many universities have student advice services, ombudspersons or student unions that can support you.

If you’re a teacher or editor

  • Treat detector scores as a signal to look closer, never as proof.
  • Compare the work with the writer’s previous work and ask to see drafts or version history.
  • Talk with the writer before reaching conclusions, and consider language background and discipline.
  • Set clear, specific AI policies for each assignment so expectations aren’t ambiguous.

The bottom line

AI detectors can offer a useful hint, but they make mistakes, and some writers are affected more than others. Good habits (version history, notes, following the rules and disclosing AI use) protect you far better than any score.
