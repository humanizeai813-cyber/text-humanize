How to write a professional email people actually read

How to write clear professional emails: subject lines that get opened, a simple structure, the right tone, polite follow-ups and before-and-after examples.

Most work emails are read quickly, often on a phone, between other tasks. A good professional email respects that: the reader can tell what it’s about from the subject line, understands what you need in the first couple of sentences, and knows exactly what to do next.

This guide covers subject lines, structure, tone, follow-ups and a final checklist, with examples you can adapt.

Write a subject line that says what the email is for

The subject line decides whether your email is opened now, later or never. Make it specific, and include any action or deadline.

VagueClear
QuestionQuestion about the invoice for order 4471
MeetingCan we move Tuesday’s design review to 3pm?
UpdateWebsite launch update: new date is 14 October
Important!!Approval needed by Friday: Q4 training budget

Keep it short enough to read in an inbox list, and update it if the conversation changes topic.

Put the purpose in the first two lines

After a short greeting, say why you’re writing. Background can follow, but the reader shouldn’t have to search for the point.

Buried purpose

Hi Sam, I hope you’re well. As you know, we’ve been working on the new onboarding materials for a few weeks now, and there have been a number of discussions about the format, and the team has put together several versions…

Purpose first

Hi Sam, could you choose between the two onboarding guide drafts by Thursday? Both are attached. Here’s a quick summary of the differences.

Use a simple structure

  1. Greeting: “Hi Priya,” works for most workplaces; “Dear Dr Okafor,” for formal first contact.
  2. Purpose: one or two sentences on why you’re writing.
  3. Details: only the context the reader needs to act.
  4. The ask: what you need, from whom, by when.
  5. Close: “Thanks,” or “Best regards,” and your name, with a signature that includes your role and contact details.

Stick to one main topic per email. If you have three unrelated questions for different people, three short emails are usually easier to act on than one long one.

Make the ask impossible to miss

Unclear requests lead to slow replies. State the action, the owner and the timing:

  • Instead of “Let me know your thoughts,” write “Could you reply with any changes by 5pm Wednesday?”
  • Instead of “Hopefully we can find a time,” write “Are you free Monday 10am or Tuesday 2pm for 30 minutes?”

If several people are copied, name who needs to do what. People tend to assume someone else will respond.

Format for skimming

  • Short paragraphs of two or three sentences
  • Bullet points for lists of items, options or steps
  • Bold only for the one thing that must not be missed, such as a deadline
  • Descriptive file names for attachments, mentioned in the email body

Get the tone right

Professional doesn’t mean stiff. Aim for polite, direct and friendly.

  • Drop excessive apologies. “Sorry to bother you, I’m so sorry if this is a silly question” can become “Quick question about the report.”
  • Swap formal filler for plain words. “Please be advised that the meeting has been rescheduled” becomes “We’ve moved the meeting to Thursday.”
  • Be careful with humour and sarcasm. Without tone of voice, they are easy to misread.
  • Match the other person. If a client writes formally, mirror that until they relax the tone.

For more on plain wording, see how to improve readability.

Write polite follow-ups

People miss emails. A follow-up after a few working days is normal, sooner if there’s a firm deadline. Keep it short and make replying easy:

Hi Jordan, just following up on the email below about the venue booking. Could you confirm by Friday whether the 12 November date works? If it’s easier, a quick yes or no is fine. Thanks!

Reply in the same thread so the history stays together, and avoid guilt-inducing lines like “As per my last email…”.

Using AI to draft emails

AI tools can save time on routine emails, especially first drafts and rewording. A few habits keep that time saving from backfiring:

  • Give the facts yourself: names, dates, amounts and the exact request.
  • Cut the generic padding AI often adds, like “I hope this email finds you well” followed by three sentences of preamble.
  • Check every detail before sending. A wrong date or name costs more time than the draft saved.
  • Don’t paste confidential information into tools your organisation hasn’t approved.

If a draft sounds robotic, the Text Humanize editor with “Writing for: Email” can suggest a more natural version while keeping names, numbers and links intact.

Before you hit send

  • The subject line says what the email is for
  • The purpose is clear in the first two lines
  • The ask includes who, what and by when
  • Names, dates, numbers and attachments are correct
  • The recipients are right, and “Reply all” is really needed
  • You would be comfortable if the email were forwarded
